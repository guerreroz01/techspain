Wing, Alphabet's drone delivery subsidiary, and Walmart will bring their service to the Denver and Seattle metros in 2027. With those two markets, the alliance the two companies have maintained for years reaches 19 U.S. metros, though actual delivery remains concentrated in very few places.

Of those 19 markets, only five have aircraft flying today: Dallas-Fort Worth, Houston, Atlanta, Charlotte and Orlando. The other fourteen are announced and waiting, twelve of them with no public launch date, according to Wing's own count. The company says more than three million customers already have access to the service.

## What a Hummingbird flies and carries

The operation rests on Wing's Hummingbird, an aircraft that cruises at up to 97 km/h, carries up to 2.3 kilograms of payload and does not land to deliver: it lowers the box into the yard on a tether. It is the same delivery format the company uses in the markets already operating.

The stated goal for 2027 is more than 270 Walmart stores and 40 million potential customers. Wing's chief business officer, Heather Rivera, tied the expansion to adoption of drone delivery, arguing that the new markets show how quickly the public is embracing it.

Neither Denver nor Seattle appeared on the lists Wing and Walmart published in January or June. Both cities surfaced in September, in urban land-use filings.

## Denver: suburban rules arrived before the announcement

The detail that orders Denver's story is not the city's selection, but that local regulation moved before the company. Castle Rock passed an emergency ordinance on July 20 freezing drone-hub applications for six months, and Parker did the same on July 21 with a nine-month pause. Both municipalities sit south of Denver.

To the north, Northglenn's Planning Commission voted on September 15 to recommend Colorado's first drone-delivery ordinance. Walmart had approached the city about flying Wing drones from its Neighborhood Market on Washington Street. The draft keeps launch pads 61 meters from homes and schools, caps noise at the property line at 65 decibels and limits hours to 7 a.m.–10 p.m. The City Council has a first reading set for October 12 and the final vote on October 26.

Thornton is drafting its own hub ordinance and Longmont holds two Walmart pre-applications with no hearing scheduled. All those rules govern the parking lot, not the air: a nest in the neighbouring town can serve any address in the city because airspace is the FAA's remit. Wing's nests have a range of about 9.7 kilometres, so the hub one locality blocks can cover its neighbour.

The case is not isolated: municipal opposition to aerial delivery has already slowed the rollout of Amazon Prime Air in other U.S. localities.

## Seattle: one Tacoma permit and Zipline ahead on paperwork

Wing's first sign in Washington was a building permit for the Tacoma Walmart Supercenter, filed in September and with no associated FAA filing. Monday's announcement quotes Federal Way mayor Jim Ferrell rather than officials from Tacoma or Seattle, and Wing appears nowhere on the FAA's public list of environmental reviews for the state.

Rival Zipline is ahead on paper. The FAA published a draft environmental assessment in September 2025 for up to 75 Zipline sites and 500 docks across the Seattle metro, took comments through November 2 that year and has issued no decision yet.

The FAA signed a nationwide programmatic environmental assessment on July 28 that could let Wing skip metro-by-metro filings. The company has not said whether it will use it.

## What it means for the rollout

The expansion to Denver and Seattle is a network move, not a technology one: the bottleneck is not the drone, but the take-off points spread across Walmart parking lots. A municipal moratorium does not stop the flight, but it does stop the pad, and without pads every 9.7 kilometres the network cannot cover a city. Every month of pause is a month of coverage Wing cannot bill, and Colorado's regulatory calendar suggests the company will have to negotiate figure by figure — altitude, noise, aircraft count and flight frequency — before the first package lifts off from a Denver parking lot.
