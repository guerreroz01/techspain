An Android phone dropping to half a charge before noon is a very common complaint,
and in most cases it comes down to avoidable causes: a screen that is too bright,
apps refreshing in the background non-stop, careless charging habits or an app that
keeps drawing power while you get on with your day. There is no need to switch to
airplane mode, settle for a screen so dim you can barely read it or turn off Wi-Fi:
a few targeted adjustments can give hours back to your day with barely any
difference in everyday use.

It is worth looking at the problem from two angles. The first is daily drain; the
second is the long-term health of the battery, because nobody wants to end up
replacing it before its time. This guide orders the adjustments into four steps and
each one spells out the real menu path, exactly as Engadget documents it.

## Prerequisites

- An Android phone with full access to the **Settings** app.
- A few minutes: the settings in this tutorial take less than five minutes to change.
- Bear in mind that menu names vary by manufacturer: routes such as **Battery
  Protection** may appear under a different title on your device.

## Steps

### 1. Tune the display, the biggest drain

The screen is almost always the largest battery drain on the phone, so that is
where to start.

- Lower the brightness manually or turn on auto-brightness. Do this in
  **Settings > Display**, in the *Brightness* section, so the display does not burn
  unnecessary power in dim environments.
- Cut the screen timeout to 30 seconds so the display does not linger when you are
  not looking at it. The setting lives in **Settings > Display > Screen timeout**.
- If your phone supports a 120 Hz refresh rate, drop it to 60 Hz for everyday use:
  in **Settings > Display > Motion smoothness** pick *Standard* over *Adaptive*.
  Scrolling and gaming will be less smooth, but it is not the end of the world. It
  is the same principle that works on a laptop with a
  [reduced screen refresh rate in Windows 11](/noticias/windows-11-pantalla-bateria/).

![Dark mode on an Android phone screen](./assets/ajustes-pantalla.jpg)

Dark mode is also an option, but results vary: on OLED or AMOLED panels, a Purdue
University study puts the display power saving between 39 and 47 percent at full
brightness, while at typical indoor brightness levels that saving shrinks to a range
of 3 to 9 percent.

### 2. Turn on Battery Saver and Adaptive Battery

Beyond the display, there are two system tools that do quiet work:

- **Battery Saver** (also called *Power Saving*) restricts activity when the charge
  drops below a threshold you set. It lives in **Battery > Battery saver** and
  offers two modes: *Standard* and *Maximum*.
- **Adaptive Battery** goes one step further: it learns which apps you actually
  open and limits power to the ones you rarely touch. Enable it at
  **Settings > Battery > Background usage limits > Three-dot menu > Adaptive battery**.

### 3. Build smarter charging habits

Charging also decides how long the battery lasts over time. Most users plug their
phone in overnight and leave it at 100 percent, and that habit quietly degrades the
cell.

- Lithium-ion batteries are healthiest between 20 and 80 percent charge. According
  to Battery University, keeping the battery in that range keeps voltage lower,
  which can significantly extend cycle life; consistently charging to 100 percent
  and leaving the phone plugged in, especially in a warm environment, accelerates
  capacity loss.
- When you can, charge to about 80 percent and unplug before bed if your phone does
  not have a built-in charge limit.
- Many current Android phones do ship that limit: **Settings > Battery > Battery
  Protection** (the name varies by brand) lets you pick a cap between 80 and 95
  percent.

![A phone charging on a desk](./assets/carga-sobremesa.jpg)

- If you would rather always see the battery at 100 percent, the *Basic* setting
  stops charging once it reaches that level and only resumes the top-up when it
  drops back to 95 percent.
- It pays to run partial charges: topping up to 70 percent is gentler on the cell
  than running it to empty and filling it back up.
- Fast charging, while convenient, generates more heat than standard charging, and
  heat is the primary cause of degradation.

For another ecosystem, the same care philosophy is covered in our guide to
[optimizing and looking after an iPhone battery](/noticias/iphone-battery-optimization-guide/).

### 4. Find out what is draining the battery

If the phone keeps losing charge faster than it should despite everything above, it
is time to check what is eating it.

1. Go to **Settings > Battery > Battery usage** and look at which apps have consumed
   the most power over the last 24 hours or longer. Watch for anything that stands
   out: a navigation app that ran in the background all day, a social network
   refreshing constantly or a game that never fully closed.
2. Tap the app's name. You will see extra information and, more importantly, a
   button to set background usage limits, which puts the app into *sleep* or *deep
   sleep*. In *sleep*, the app runs in the background occasionally and its updates
   and notifications are delayed; in *deep sleep* it never runs in the background.
3. Rule out malware as well: another possible cause of an abnormal drain is an
   infected phone, spyware included.

![An app listing in the Play Store on an Android phone](./assets/consumo-apps.jpg)

4. If none of that changes the situation, run a battery test. On Samsung, open the
   **Members** app, go to **Support > Phone diagnostics** and run the scan; then
   check the battery status. Other phones use a different path, such as
   **Settings > Battery > Battery Health**, but the point is the same.

## How to verify it worked

- Go back to **Settings > Battery > Battery usage** the next day and compare the
  consumption ranking with the one from before the changes: the apps you limited
  should appear further down.
- Check that *Adaptive Battery* is still enabled under **Settings > Battery >
  Background usage limits**.
- Run the diagnostics test again: the battery status should read `Normal` (or
  `Good`, depending on the brand).
- In real use, the goal is for the phone to last the day without dropping to half
  charge at noon.

## Warnings and limitations

- Route names vary by manufacturer: **Battery Protection**, for example, is not
  called the same on every model, and the diagnostics in the **Members** app are
  exclusive to Samsung.
- Dropping from 120 Hz to 60 Hz is noticeable in scrolling and gaming smoothness.
- Dark mode savings depend on brightness: at normal indoor levels they hover around
  3–9 percent, far from the figures reached at full brightness.
- Apps in *sleep* delay notifications and updates, and *deep sleep* apps never run
  in the background: if you need an app to stay alive at all times (messaging,
  navigation), do not restrict it.
- Fast charging raises the temperature, and heat degrades the battery.
- If the diagnostics return anything other than `Normal` or `Good`, the battery may
  need replacing: no software setting recovers a cell that is already worn.
